Charity Origins

Born from the Storm: The Story of The Friends

On the night of 15–16 October 1987, hurricane-force winds swept across southern England in what became known as the Great Storm. Tunbridge Wells and Rusthall Commons were among the hardest hit landscapes in Kent. Hundreds of ancient oak, beech, and sweet chestnut trees were uprooted overnight, historical pathways were blocked, and the landscape was left in devastation.

In the days that followed, local residents from Tunbridge Wells and Rusthall came out with hand tools, wheelbarrows, and determination to help clear paths, care for damaged veteran trees, and begin replanting.

What started as an urgent community rescue effort quickly revealed a deeper truth: the Commons needed a permanent, dedicated body of local supporters to champion their ecology, fund conservation enhancements, and ensure they would never be neglected.

In 1991, following the destruction caused by the Great Storm of 1987, Patrick Shovelton (now sadly passed) founded The Friends to support the Commons Conservators in their work to restore them to their former glory. His aim was to raise public awareness of the importance of the Commons and rally the community to care for them.

The Society was founded for:

“... the conservation, maintenance, preservation and peaceful enjoyment for the benefit of the public of the Commons by such means as the Society in consultation with the Conservators for the Commons thinks fit.”

Over the past three decades, the Friends have proudly continued Patrick's wonderful work: funding tractor and habitat equipment, restoring heritage ponds, running regular volunteer working parties, publishing free trail guides, and now leading the historic community bid to secure the freehold for all.

Our Partnership with the Commons Conservators

The statutory day-to-day management of the Commons lies with the Commons Conservators, constituted by Act of Parliament in 1890 comprising representatives of the Freeholder, the Town Council, and Rusthall Parish Council. The Conservators employ the Warden and Ranger team (including the Commons Ranger, Daniel Colborne). The Friends fund enhancements outside their statutory budget, such as specialist surveys, trail restoration, and volunteer working parties.

Landscape Evolution • 256 Acres of Wildlife Refuge

A Living Mosaic: How Human History Shaped Our Commons

The 256 acres of Tunbridge Wells and Rusthall Commons provide an important and most unusual refuge for wildlife within our urban area. Unlike traditional municipal parks, they have never been landscaped or cultivated. They owe much of their present appearance to centuries of human activity, and active management is required to maintain them in a fashion beneficial to their natural inhabitants.

Deliberate planting of commemorative and ornamental trees—principally between 1860 and 1940—led unintentionally to the re-establishment of woodland as the ancient practice of livestock grazing declined and finally ceased altogether in the inter-war period of the twentieth century.

Today, the Commons consist of an extraordinary mosaic of habitats: surviving tracts of heath and acid grassland coexist with open sandy clearings around major Ardingly sandstone outcrops, secondary woodland, and semi-natural ponds like the Marlpit. Current conservation management aims to preserve this rich diversity by containing woodland encroachment and expanding open heath—enabling long-established resident butterflies, dragonflies, and birdlife to flourish.
